## Background

The post-Newtonian (PN) approximation expands the two-body problem of general relativity in powers of v^2/c^2 ~ GM/(rc^2). In the Arnowitt-Deser-Misner (ADM) gauge, the conservative orbital dynamics of a non-spinning compact binary is generated by a Hamiltonian in reduced canonical variables: Q is the relative position divided by GM, P is the relative momentum divided by the reduced mass mu, and time is measured in units of GM/c^3. With G = M = 1 the mass dependence enters only through the symmetric mass ratio eta = m1*m2/M^2, which lies in (0, 1/4], and the formal expansion parameter eps = 1/c^2 labels the PN orders. The Newtonian part H_N = P^2/2 - 1/r is the Kepler problem. The 1PN and 2PN corrections contain products of P^2, (N.P)^2 and powers of 1/r, where N = Q/r. These terms mix momenta and positions, so the full Hamiltonian cannot be written as T(P) + V(Q).

Symplectic integrators keep the energy error bounded over long times without secular drift, which is why they are preferred for inspiral and chaos studies. Explicit symplectic splitting normally needs the Hamiltonian to break into pieces whose flows are known exactly. For a non-separable Hamiltonian, one route is to double the phase space: introduce auxiliary copies (x, y) of (p, q), write an extended Hamiltonian on (p, q, x, y) whose pieces each freeze half the variables, and integrate with a leapfrog. The copies drift apart over time, so each step combines them back into a single state through a mixing or projection map. Near-integrable systems of the form H_0 + eps*H_1 add a subtlety. When the extension or the splitting mixes H_0 and eps*H_1 unevenly, error terms that scale with high powers of eps survive in the modified Hamiltonian. The observed convergence order then falls below the design order once eps is no longer small compared with the step size.

The exact Kepler flow can be advanced for any time interval, positive or negative, with the universal-variable formulation. The universal anomaly chi solves a scalar Kepler equation through Stumpff-type functions, and the new state follows from Lagrange f and g coefficients, so one formula covers elliptic, parabolic and hyperbolic arcs. Yoshida's triple-jump raises a symmetric second-order map S(h) to fourth order as S(lambda*h) S((1 - 2*lambda)*h) S(lambda*h). Because lambda > 1/2, the middle weight is negative, so some sub-flows run backward in time.
