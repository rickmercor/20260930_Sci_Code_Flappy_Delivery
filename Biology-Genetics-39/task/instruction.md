## Background

Rooted triples are among the smallest units of phylogenetic evidence that a sequence alignment can support. A triple $xy|z$ asserts only that two of three taxa share a more recent history with each other than either does with the third, and such assertions can be read off pairwise sequence dissimilarities without estimating branch lengths, substitution rates, or a fully resolved topology. Because they are cheap to obtain and comparatively robust, triples have become a standard intermediate representation: rather than fitting a global history to the alignment directly, one extracts a large collection of three-taxon statements and then asks which single evolutionary history, if any, is compatible with all of them simultaneously. For rooted trees this programme is classical, and the tree-building algorithm of Aho, Sagiv, Szymanski and Ullman decides the question in polynomial time and returns a witness whenever one exists.

Trees, however, cannot represent hybridisation, introgression, recombination or horizontal gene transfer, all of which merge lineages that had previously separated. The object that records such a history is a rooted phylogenetic network, a directed acyclic graph with a single root whose leaves are the observed taxa and whose internal vertices may have more than one parent and on such graphs the notion of "the history shows this triple" splits in two. One reading asks for internally vertex-disjoint paths realizing the triple's shape; the other compares the relative depth of least common ancestors, requiring the coalescence of the inner pair to lie strictly below the coalescence that either of them shares with the outgroup. On trees the two readings coincide, and the classical literature is free to use whichever is convenient. On networks they diverge sharply: two leaves may have several incomparable minimal common ancestors and therefore no well-defined least common ancestor at all, so the ancestor-based reading carries a well-definedness requirement with no counterpart in tree phylogenetics. The consequence is that a single network can satisfy the path-based reading for all three triples on one three-taxon subset, while under the ancestor-based reading it commits to at most one of them. Since pairwise dissimilarity is evidence about relative ancestral depth rather than about disjoint paths, it is the ancestor-based reading that matches what the data actually say, and it is the reading that has received far less algorithmic attention.

Recasting triple statements as comparisons between least common ancestors turns network reconstruction into a realization problem for order constraints on pairs of leaves: given a relation that specifies which pairwise coalescences must lie below which others, decide whether some directed acyclic graph realizes it, and construct a canonical witness when one exists. Recent work characterises the realizable relations through a closure operator generated by a small set of elementary inference rules, one of which is specific to least common ancestors rather than to orders in general, and shows that the canonical witness is the diagram of the partial order obtained by collapsing mutually dependent leaf pairs. Real analyses, though, deliver negative evidence as well as positive evidence: a reconciliation may exclude a particular coalescence order, and gene-tree conflict may rule out that one pair merges below another. This motivates a two-sided version of the problem in which required constraints are accompanied by forbidden ones, and the two sides interact rather than acting independently, a forbidden statement is discharged either by forcing coalescences to coincide or by deliberately leaving a least common ancestor ambiguous, and which repair is available depends on what the required statements already pin down. Beyond solvability, the interesting quantity is how much resolution survives: how much of the full three-taxon space the reconstructed network commits to, compared with what the positive evidence alone would have produced.

## Problem

Comparative genomics delivers evidence about rooted evolutionary histories one three-taxon subset at a time: a rooted triple $x\,y|z$ records that lineages $x$ and $y$ share a more recent history with each other than either does with $z$, and pairwise sequence dissimilarity supports exactly that kind of statement without committing to branch lengths or a resolved global topology. Hybridisation and horizontal transfer make the history a rooted phylogenetic network rather than a tree, and on a network the classical reading of a triple by internally vertex-disjoint paths parts company with the reading by relative ancestral depth; the second is what dissimilarity actually measures, so throughout, a rooted directed acyclic graph displays $x\,y|z$ when $\mathrm{lca}(xy)$, $\mathrm{lca}(xz)$ and $\mathrm{lca}(yz)$ are each a unique vertex, the latter two are the same vertex, and $\mathrm{lca}(xy)$ is a proper descendant of it, a condition that lets a reticulate history resolve at most one of the three triples on any three-taxon subset, and possibly none. Given a set of required and a set of forbidden rooted triples on a leaf set, the method returns a rooted phylogenetic network that displays every required triple and no forbidden one, and the quantity of interest here is how much of the full three-taxon space that network resolves.

The computation turns on three things: the translation of triple statements into order constraints between the least common ancestors of the one- and two-element subsets of the leaf set, the closure that exposes every constraint those already force, and the minimal adjustment that discharges the forbidden statements without costing any required one. The canonical directed acyclic graph determined by the resulting constraint relation, completed into a single-rooted phylogenetic network, is the reconstruction.
