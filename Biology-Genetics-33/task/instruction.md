# Biology-Genetics-33

## Background

Quantitative genetics describes the inheritance of many traits at once through a small number of covariance matrices, of which the additive genetic covariance matrix is the one that governs evolutionary response. What matters about it in practice is rarely the matrix itself but its spectrum: the largest eigenvalues say how fast the population can move, and the directions carrying no genetic variance at all say where it cannot move however hard selection pushes. Phenotyping platforms now return thousands of traits from a single breeding design, so the number of traits is comparable to, or larger than, the number of individuals that can be measured, and the classical likelihood machinery of the field was built for a handful of traits and does not survive that regime; it becomes computationally prohibitive and, worse, its eigenvalue estimates spread apart in a way that inflates the apparent dimensionality of the very null space the study is meant to detect.

The response has been to stop estimating the matrix and estimate its eigenvalue distribution instead, giving that distribution a parametric form with a few unknowns and fitting those unknowns by matching moments. The statistics available are the sums of squares that the breeding design itself produces, one for each level of the design, and their trace moments have the useful property that in the high-dimensional regime they stop fluctuating and converge to deterministic functions of the design and of the unknown parameters. Matching the observed moments to those deterministic functions turns a hard optimisation into the inversion of a small map, and for some designs the levels can be handled one at a time rather than all at once.

Every construction of this kind rests on assumptions about the covariance components that the data do not directly verify, and the one that carries the most weight is the assumption that the different components are diagonalised by the same set of eigenvectors. It is a convenience rather than a biological claim: genetic and environmental sources of variation are not obliged to align their principal axes, and there is evidence in several systems that they do not. When the assumption fails, the moment map that the fit inverts is not the map the data were generated by, and the parameters it returns are shifted by an amount that no diagnostic internal to the fit will reveal, because the fitted moments still reproduce the observed ones exactly. Whether that shift is negligible or large enough to change a conclusion about how constrained a population is depends on the design, on the spectra and on the angle between the two eigenbases, and it is a deterministic question rather than a statistical one: the limiting moments are exact, so the discrepancy can be computed rather than simulated.

## Problem

The additive genetic covariance matrix $G$ of a multi-trait population decides which combinations of traits can respond to selection at all, and the fraction of trait directions on which it carries no variance is the hard constraint on evolution; recovering that fraction, rather than the whole matrix, is usually what a breeding or evolutionary study is after. When the number of traits is comparable to the number of individuals, likelihood fits of $G$ are both computationally out of reach and badly biased for exactly those spectral features, and the practical alternative is to give the eigenvalue distribution of each variance component a low-dimensional parametric form and fit it by matching the trace moments of the sums of squares the design produces to the deterministic values those moments take in the high-dimensional limit. I want to know what that fitting procedure reports for my study when one of its standard working assumptions is false.

My design is a full-sibling study with $I = 300$ families measured on $p = 240$ traits. Family $i$, for $i = 1, \dots, 300$, holds $1$, $2$ or $3$ offspring according to whether $i \bmod 3$ is $1$, $2$ or $0$, so there are $n = 600$ individuals in all, and an unknown population mean common to every individual is carried as a fixed effect. The family-level component $\Sigma_1$ has a single nonzero eigenvalue $\tau_1 = 1.4$ carried by $\lceil p\rho \rceil$ trait directions with $\rho = 0.35$ and zero on the remainder; the individual-level component $\Sigma_2$ has a single nonzero eigenvalue $\tau_2 = 0.6$ carried by $\lceil p\rho_0 \rceil$ directions with $\rho_0 = 0.8$ known in advance, and its eigenvectors are the trait coordinate axes. Both components list their eigenvalues in nonincreasing order, so in each case the nonzero ones sit on the leading directions of that component's own eigenbasis. The two components do not share an eigenbasis: the eigenvectors of $\Sigma_1$ are obtained from the coordinate axes by a plane rotation of angle $\omega = \pi/5$ inside each mirrored coordinate pair $(i,\, p+1-i)$, so that each eigenvector keeps weight $\cos\omega$ on its own coordinate axis and weight $\pm\sin\omega$ on its mirror.

Fit all three unknown parameters together, matching the first three trace moments of the between-family sum-of-squares matrix to the values the working model implies for them, and using throughout the working assumption that the two components are simultaneously diagonalisable with the family-level spectrum supported inside the residual one. The resulting system is polynomial and is not inverted in closed form; exactly one of its solutions has both eigenvalues positive and the fitted genetic fraction strictly below $\rho_0$, and locating that one to a relative accuracy of $10^{-12}$ fixes every number below. Work in the exact high-dimensional limit, replacing every empirical trace moment by the deterministic value it converges to almost surely for this design and these components, so that what you report is a limit and not a sample estimate. Your final answer must be a single number, the fitted fraction $\hat\rho$ of trait directions the procedure reports as carrying nonzero additive genetic variance, quoted to at least six significant figures and obtained exactly rather than by simulating datasets. State, in one line each, the rule that fixes the almost-sure limit of a trace moment from the design and the two covariance components, the matrix with which the between-family sum of squares is formed, and the places in the fit at which the shared-eigenbasis assumption is false; then report the fitted family-level eigenvalue, the fitted individual-level eigenvalue, the three limiting trace moments of the between-family sum-of-squares matrix, the normalised trace of the product of the two true components, the values $\hat\rho$ takes at $\omega = 0$, $\pi/10$, $\pi/4$, $\pi/3$ and $\pi/2$ with everything else held fixed, the value it takes when the family sizes cycle $2, 3, 4$ instead of $1, 2, 3$, the value it takes when the study is enlarged to $600$ families on the same size cycle, and what those trends say about inferring the rank of $G$ from such a fit.
